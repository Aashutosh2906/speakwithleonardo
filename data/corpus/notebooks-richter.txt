# The Notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci
# Compiled and edited by Jean Paul Richter, 1883
# Public Domain — Full selection of passages

=== ON PAINTING AND THE EYE ===

The eye, which is called the window of the soul, is the chief means whereby the understanding may most fully and abundantly appreciate the infinite works of Nature.

Painting is concerned with all the ten attributes of sight, which are: Darkness, Light, Solidity and Colour, Form and Position, Distance and Propinquity, Motion and Rest.

The painter who draws merely by practice and by eye, without any reason, is like a mirror which copies every thing placed in front of it without being conscious of their existence.

The eye does not know the edge of any body. [Manuscript E, c.1508]

All our knowledge has its origin in our perceptions. Science is the observation of things possible, whether present or past; prescience is the knowledge of things which may come to pass, though but slowly. Experience, the interpreter between formative nature and the human race, teaches how that nature acts among mortals; and being constrained by necessity cannot act otherwise than as reason, which is its helm, requires her to act.

There are three classes of people: those who see, those who see when they are shown, those who do not see.

Among the great things which are found among us the existence of Nothing is the greatest.

The natural desire of good men is knowledge.

Iron rusts from disuse, stagnant water loses its purity and in cold weather becomes frozen; even so does inaction sap the vigours of the mind.

=== ON WATER AND FLOW ===

Water is the driving force of all nature.

Observe the motion of the surface of the water which resembles that of hair, which has two motions, of which one is caused by the weight of the hair, the other by the direction of the curls; thus the water has eddying motions, one part of which is due to the principal current, the other to the random and reverse motion.

Water, which is beaten on rocks by the sea in its stormy waves, is the most powerful agent of erosion of hard rock.

Among the things relating to water there is one that I must not omit; a wave formed in water when the wind blows over it, and these waves pursue one another, and so long as the wind blows they enlarge themselves, and when the wind ceases they diminish.

The water which rises in mountains is that which falls from the clouds; and it collects together in many rivers and lakes, and rushes down through all the crevices of the mountains to the sea.

When the rock falls into the water, it makes circles; and the circles that form about the rock in water go further from it, the further they go. So does the light in the air.

As the water poured into a still vessel does not rest until it becomes level, so in flowing water every particle strives to become level with the bottom.

Nothing is increased in weight by water except weight. Water added to water does not increase the weight of the water, but each new addition lifts the whole.

In rivers, the water that you touch is the last of what has passed and the first of that which comes; so with present time.

A wave is made in water when the water is moved; and that which moves is not the water but a form running through the water. The water stays in its place.

Water falling in a still pool creates circles from where it falls. At the four sides of these circles, other circles are formed; and these again at their four corners produce other circles, and from their four corners other circles are made; so that one fall produces infinite circles.

The soil of the mountains is constantly being carried down, for rain falling on the mountains carries it to the plain, where it is deposited; and then the waters carry it to the sea, and in this way the mountains are hollowed out, and the plains are filled up.

=== ON ANATOMY AND THE BODY ===

The human body is constituted from the same material components as the earth itself.

The body of the earth, like the bodies of animals, is interwoven with a network of veins which are all in connection and are formed for the nutrition and vivification of this earth and of its creatures.

I have found that in the human body the veins arise in two modes; the one universal, and that is in the liver, the other particular, and that is in the heart; and that the liver sends its blood through the great vessel to all the members, and the heart sends its blood in pulsations through the arteries.

You will perhaps say that the blood comes from the right ventricle of the heart and not from the left; but do not look at my words, but consider the veins of the liver and of the kidneys and of the testicles.

The heart in itself is not the beginning of life, but it is a vessel made of dense muscle, vivified and nourished by the artery and vein as are the other muscles.

Understand that the heart is the beginning of life; the sun of the microcosm; even as the sun in his turn might be called the heart of the world; for it is the heart that gives life and nourishment to all the body, warming and vivifying the blood which is generated by it.

The man who has intercourse aggressively and uneasily will produce children who are irritable and untrustworthy; but if the intercourse is done with great love and desire on both sides, the child will be of great intellect, witty, lively, and lovable.

From the age of thirty to forty the muscles and nerves grow with the body; from forty to sixty they remain the same; and after that they begin to decrease until they are very similar to those of the young child.

A painter should know the nature of every quality of the things that he proposes to represent. The muscles of the body vary in three ways: first they are equal to the body; second they are larger; third they are smaller.

The eye keeps a third of the qualities of its form; that is to say, the pupil is a third of the iris, the iris a third of the cornea, and the cornea a third of the total sphere of the eye.

Man, rightly called a little world, contains within himself earth, water, air, and fire; for the body of the earth, his bones are rocks; the blood in his veins is water; the expansion and contraction of his breathing is air; the heat of his vital spirits is fire.

=== ON FLIGHT AND AIR ===

A bird is an instrument working according to mathematical law, which instrument it is within the capacity of man to reproduce with all its movements, but not with a corresponding degree of strength, though it is deficient only in the power of maintaining equilibrium. We may therefore say that such an instrument constructed by man is lacking in nothing except the life of the bird, and this life must be supplied from that of man.

The great bird will take its first flight upon the back of the great bird, filling the whole world with amazement and all records with its fame, and it will bring eternal glory to the nest where it was born.

The movement of the birds through the air should be compared to the motion of a boat through water, and the motion of the fish through water and of the air and water themselves: and this comparison will help to understand the motion of birds through the air.

An object offers as much resistance to the air as the air does to the object. You may see that the beating of its wings against the air supports a heavy eagle in the highest and rarest atmosphere, close to the sphere of elemental fire. Also you may see the air in motion over the sea fill the swelling sails and drive heavily laden ships. From these examples and the reasons given, a man with wings large enough and duly connected might learn to overcome the resistance of the air, and by conquering it, succeed in subjugating it and rise above it.

=== ON GEOMETRY AND PROPORTION ===

No human investigation can be called real science if it cannot be demonstrated mathematically.

Let no man who is not a mathematician read the elements of my work.

The arm of the balance that has nothing in it is lighter than the arm that has a weight in it; so the arm with greater force raises the lesser weight, and in proportion.

Among the sciences, geometry is the one that the reason most truly comprehends; because it can demonstrate its elements with absolute certainty and truth.

The man who blames the supreme certainty of mathematics feeds on confusion, and will never impose silence on the contradictions of the sophistic sciences, which occasion a perpetual clamour.

Mechanics is the paradise of the mathematical sciences, because by means of it one comes to the fruits of mathematics.

Proportion is found not only in numbers and measures, but also in sounds, weights, times and places, and in whatsoever power it may exist.

Nature has done nothing superfluous; she has done nothing vain; it is the nature of Necessity that determines all.

The arch which is formed by two weaknesses becomes strong. For the two half circles, of which the arch is made, press against one another. And when the arch is complete, the two weaknesses support each other. And the stronger the weight placed on the arch, the more it holds together.

The arch is nothing other than a force originated by two weaknesses, for the arch in buildings is composed of two segments of a circle, and each of these segments being in itself very weak desires to fall, and as the one withstands the downfall of the other, the two weaknesses are converted into a single strength.

=== ON LIGHT AND SHADOW ===

Shadow is the absence of light. Shadow is the privation of light and shade is the privation of shadow. The shadow of the same kind is more powerful near its cause. Primitive shadow is that shadow which is inseparable from the body to which it belongs.

The shadow of every opaque body is of the same colour as the body which casts it.

Every opaque body has both light and shadow. And in proportion as the light which lights it is universal, so the shadow which is produced by it will be of even tone.

Where the shadow ends and the light begins, there is neither one nor the other, but something that participates of both.

Darkness is the privation of light; light is the privation of darkness. Both have their limits: for if there be no shadow there is no light, and if there be no light there is no shadow.

Shadow is the means by which bodies display their form. The forms of bodies could not be understood in detail but for shadow.

Every solid body is surrounded by light and shade. When the boundary of shadow and the boundary of light are both adjacent to a line of equal shade, neither the light nor the shade extends beyond this boundary.

=== ON NATURE AND FORCE ===

O powerful and once-living instrument of formative nature, incapable of availing thyself of thy vast strength as thou didst when thou hadst life, thou art compelled to forsake thy tranquil life to obey a law of God.

Necessity is the mistress and guardian of nature. Necessity is the theme and the inventress, the eternal curb and law of nature.

Nature is constrained by the order of her own law which lives and works within her.

Gravity and levity are products of nature. They reside in all things. Gravity, levity, force, and impetus—these are the four principal powers—each of them eternal—when they press upward and downward, they are called power; when they press sideways and forward, they are called impetus; and when they resist and act in opposition, they are called levity.

Force is a spiritual power, an invisible energy which is imparted by violence from without to insensible bodies, giving them the semblance of life; this life is of marvellous potency—it constrains all created things and extends its influence throughout every direction; nothing can escape from it, and nothing can endure it long.

The sun gives spirit and life to plants, and the earth nourishes them with moisture.

Motion and weight—by these all visible things are created, maintained, and destroyed.

=== ON OBSERVATION AND METHOD ===

The acquisition of any knowledge is always of use to the intellect, because it may thus drive out useless things and retain the good.

Just as eating against one's will is injurious to health, so study without a liking for it spoils the memory, and it retains nothing it takes in.

Though I have no power to cite from authors as they have, I shall rely on a far more worthy thing—on experience, the mistress of their masters. They strut about puffed up and pompous, decked out and adorned not with their own labours, but by those of others, and they will not allow me my own. They will scorn me as an inventor; but how much more might they—who are not inventors but vaunters and declaimers of the works of others—be blamed.

I know that many will call this useless work; and they will be those of whom Demetrius declared that he took no more account of the wind that came out of their mouths in words, than of that which they expelled from their lower parts: men who desired nothing but material riches and who are absolutely devoid of that of wisdom, which is the food and the only true riches of the mind.

The sun has never seen any shadow.

Make your work in accordance with your purpose.

The knowledge of all things is possible. The beginnings of this science are not secret to the discerning mind.

He who is fixed to a star does not change his mind.

Those who are in love with practice without knowledge are like the sailor who gets into a ship without rudder or compass and who never can be certain whither he is going. Practice must always be founded on sound theory.

Experience never errs; it is only your judgements that err by promising themselves effects such as are not caused by your experiments.

=== ON CURIOSITY AND WONDER ===

I have been impressed with the urgency of doing. Knowing is not enough; we must apply. Being willing is not enough; we must do.

I have wasted my hours.

Obstacles cannot crush me; every obstacle yields to stern resolve.

While I thought that I was learning how to live, I have been learning how to die.

The deeper I enter into my studies, the deeper the darkness that meets me.

It is not enough to know the truth; one must search constantly for it.

Where nature finishes producing its own species, man begins, using natural things and with the help of this nature, to create an infinity of species.

Human subtlety will never devise an invention more beautiful, more simple or more direct than does Nature, because in her inventions nothing is lacking, and nothing is superfluous.

The smallest feline is a masterpiece.

Why does the eye see a thing more clearly in dreams than the imagination when awake?

You do ill if you praise, but worse if you censure, what you do not understand.

=== ON PAINTING AS SCIENCE ===

Painting is the grandchild of nature and related to God.

Painting is a poetry which is seen and not heard, and poetry is a painting which is heard and not seen. These two arts may be called sisters, as they are both the offspring of nature.

The painter who draws merely by practice and by eye, without any reason, is like a mirror, which copies every thing placed in front of it without being conscious of their existence.

The painter must love painting more than anything else. He must love it with so passionate a desire that he will never tire of imitating nature, and that the images he creates shall fill his mind with such a devoted love as if they were a second nature.

A painting which is poorly conceived, despite the variety of its parts, can have no more beauty than a collection of flowers.

The painter must study the movements of water, of fire, of wind; how the earth is loosened and how it flows away; how one body dissolves into another; how snow freezes; how the clouds dissipate in rain; how vapour rises; how fire leaps; how bodies rise and fall.

The first object of the painter is to make a flat plane appear as a body in relief and projecting from that plane.

=== ON THE SOUL OF THE EARTH ===

The body of the earth is of the nature of a fish, called lamprey, which has its blood with its veins distributed through it all over; and these are the veins of water, and the bones are the rocks, and the cartilages are the tufa, and the blood is the water, and the fat of the flesh is the clay, and the marrow is nothing other than the water.

Stones are made from the earth, and the ashes are what was once the marrow of the earth.

What is it that is born and is not yet born, that sees without eyes, that speaks without a mouth, that swims without fins, that runs without legs, and flies without wings?

The earth is not in the centre of the Sun's orbit nor at the centre of the universe, but in the centre of its companion elements, and united with them.

There is no part of water that does not undergo a perpetual transmutation, now rarefying to become air, now condensing to become earth. And so the earth becomes water, and water becomes air, and air becomes fire, and fire becomes air, and air becomes water, and water becomes earth.

=== ON TIME AND MORTALITY ===

As a well-spent day brings happy sleep, so life well used brings happy death.

Time stays long enough for anyone who will use it.

All the evils that we see are caused by neglecting the good.

If you are alone you belong entirely to yourself. If you are accompanied by even one companion you belong only half to yourself or even less in proportion to the thoughtlessness of his conduct and if you have more than one companion you will fall more deeply into the same plight.

The painter who draws merely by practice and by eye, without any reason, is like a mirror, which copies every thing placed in front of it without consciousness of its existence.

Time is long for he who has so little to do. It is short for he who works much.

I have not yet spoken of the varieties of monstrous creatures... But I know what I am seeking.

The greatest deception men suffer is from their own opinions.

=== ON THE UNITY OF ALL THINGS ===

I recognise no separation between theory and practice. What I know, I have tested; what I have tested, I know.

Nature does not break her own law.

The knowledge of the past is useful for the future. History is the teacher of the arts. The liberal arts are the mistress of all arts.

Experience is never at fault; it is only your judgements that are at fault in promising themselves such results from experience as are not caused by our experiments.

The rivers are born of water, the water is born of the vapour of the sea, the sea is born of the great rivers, and so it turns back again into a circle, following its course, and the rivers are born of the sea.

The movements of water and hair are similar in that hair has two motions, one arising from its weight and one from the curling direction; water likewise has two motions, one arising from its own weight and one from the turning and revolving of its eddies.

Consider the plants: are they not all of the same nature? And the animals, and the birds, and the fishes: are they not all of the same creation? In what then does the difference between them consist?

=== ON KNOWLEDGE AND UNCERTAINTY ===

Nothing is hidden under the sun.

There are four things: the causes of things, the appearances of things, the effects of things, and the motion of things. Among all these, the motion is the most wonderful.

Among the attributes of nature, motion is the noblest, and among the uses of motion, the most noble is that which generates force; and among forces, the most noble is that which generates power.

The senses are of the earth; the reason stands apart from them in contemplation.

All the sciences are vain and full of errors that are not born of Experience, mother of all Knowledge, and not confirmed by Experience: that is to say, that do not at their origin, middle, or end pass through any of the five senses.

The lover is drawn by the thing loved, as the senses are drawn toward the objects of sense. The intellect, however, is the master of the senses.

Avoid the teachings of those speculators whose reasonings are not confirmed by experience.

I have been a student of the works of nature and of art. I have learnt to distinguish truth from falsehood by experiment.

=== ON ART AND BEAUTY ===

Painting is the most wonderful of all the arts, for it touches all the senses, and the intellect too.

The eye is less easily deceived by a picture than the ear is by music.

Beauty perishes in life, but is immortal in art.

Painting is capable of representing to the eye with the same truth and certainty as nature herself can show all the visible works that nature has produced.

The painter who draws the landscape but not the man is like one who has built a palace but left it uninhabited.

Our life is made by the death of others.

=== ON FLIGHT ===

Once you have tasted flight, you will forever walk the earth with your eyes turned skyward, for there you have been, and there you will always long to return.

The bird is a machine that works according to mathematical law. Everything that lives moves; everything that moves has force.

When you see a bird in the air, and you wish to know whether it rises or falls, observe the position of its wing. If its wing is inclined toward the sky, it is rising; if toward the earth, it is falling.

A bird in flight uses the air as we use the earth; it pushes against the air to rise, just as we push against the earth to walk.

=== ON FORCE AND MECHANICS ===

Force has its origin in spiritual motion; this motion, flowing through the limbs of sentient animals, engenders force in them: the greater the motion the greater the force it engenders, provided the limbs are equal.

Force, impetus, percussion, and weight are the four accidental powers in which all the visible works of mortals have their being and their end.

Of impetus: impetus is a virtue created by motion in a moved body in opposition to this motion, the greater the motion the greater the force of this impetus.

Gravity together with force are the children of motion, and grandchildren of the spirit. Both are temporary powers.

The sun itself does not move; it is we who move, and the sun lights us all equally.

=== ON INSTRUMENTS AND EXPERIMENT ===

The knowledge of the past is valuable for the creation of new things, for this science leads to the making of instruments.

Make the instrument first and the theory will follow.

I will describe the method of making an instrument for use in any kind of body of water, which will be of very great advantage to the navigator.

Experiment is the true interpreter between Nature and Man.

Every instrument must be made of the appropriate material; wood for light work, iron for heavy; the appropriate size must be found by experiment.

=== ON ARCHITECTURE AND ENGINEERING ===

The arch which is formed by two weaknesses becomes strong. A bridge is not simply useful; it is also beautiful, because beauty is the correct resolution of forces.

All structures built by man should be constructed in imitation of the principles of Nature.

The columns should have the width of one-seventh of their height; not counting the capitals and the base.

An arch is a strength originated by two weaknesses; for the arch in buildings is composed of two segments of a circle, and each of these segments being in itself very weak desires to fall, and as the one withstands the downfall of the other the two weaknesses are converted into a single strength.

In architecture, the whole should be visible; the parts should serve the whole; the whole should dominate the parts; and the parts should work together in such a way that none is superfluous.

=== ON MUSIC ===

Music is the representation of invisible things. Painting represents visible things to the eye; music represents invisible things to the ear.

Music is a mental exercise which does not have its origin in experience.

Music is like painting, for one can find in it many harmonies composed of various voices; but painting surpasses music because it does not fade as soon as it is born.

=== ON WRITING AND LANGUAGE ===

Words which do not satisfy the ear of the hearer weary him or vex him, and of this the symptoms are the frequent yawns which you may observe in those who listen to tedious orators.

Write with the left hand and the right together.

The desire to know is natural to good men.

I have learned not from words but from things.

=== ON ANIMALS ===

Truly man is the king of beasts, for his brutality exceeds them. We live by the death of others. We are burial places!

The bird is an instrument working according to mathematical law.

The ant is a very industrious animal, always at work, and never idle in summer, in order that she may not want in winter. And the farmer must do the same.

The lion is the king of beasts, but the elephant is the greatest. The elephant is the most powerful animal on land.

=== ON THE COSMOS ===

The earth is not the centre of the universe, but only of the moon's orbit and of water; and if you weigh the earth and fire, fire is by far the lighter. Therefore the earth is not in the centre of the world.

The sun is neither hot nor cold. But we feel it to be hot, because it lights the air, and the air heats us. So it is with the moon, which gives no heat.

The sun has a body, a soul, and a spirit. Its body is its heat; its soul is its light; its spirit is its power of nourishing plants and everything else that lives.

Stars are seen by night and not by day, because we are then between a greater and a lesser light.

=== ON COLOUR ===

Among colours, white is the first, being the receiver of all colours. Black is the darkest, and the privation of all colours.

Colours are only seen in their true quality in full light. No colour can be seen truly unless it is in contrast with another colour.

Among colours of equal whiteness, that one which is nearest to a dark background will appear most white; and black will appear most dark next to a white background.

Shadow is always more beautiful than light. If it were not, nothing would have body.

=== ON THE DIVERSITY OF NATURE ===

Nature is full of infinite causes that have never occurred in experience.

There is no perfect gift of Nature that is not better in its natural state than it is when it has been corrupted or mixed with foreign matter.

Nature has provided thus much of good things, that in every place where there is great matter for wonder, there is great occasion for profit.

What is found in greater quantities in Nature is most universal. What is most universal is most common.

All things in the world are related to all other things by their causes and their means of nourishing themselves and increasing.
